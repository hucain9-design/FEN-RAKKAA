Strategic Project Development Proposal

Project Fen Rakkaa — Governing the US Dollar Exposure of Male' Water and Sewerage Company

Hussain Shaany · S2610358 · Lecturer: Mr. Hussain Haleem · 13 August 2026 · Word count 4,416

1. Introduction

This document presents a strategic project development proposal for Male' Water and Sewerage Company (MWSC). The Business Plan 2026 identifies the USD exchange rate crisis as its top corporate challenge: MWSC earns in Maldivian Rufiyaa (MVR) but pays for imports in US Dollars (USD), and the widening gap now feeds directly into its cost base. The proposal analyses the gaps behind this exposure and proposes a project to close them.

The proposed project, Fen Rakkaa ("safeguarding water"), is a twelve-month, MVR 4.5 million programme with three thrusts: earn revenue in dollars, cut dollar spending, and set clear rules for foreign exchange (FX) risk.

The report identifies the organisation and the project's strategic importance, presents the proposal document, then evaluates three options, recommends the most feasible, and develops a Logical Framework Matrix (Logframe) and Risk Management Plan for it.

2. Organisation and Case for Change

2.1 Organisational Context

Established in 1995, MWSC is the pioneer water and sewerage utility in the Maldives and a state-owned enterprise, 80% owned by the Government and 20% by Hitachi, with a total of 267,000 shares (MWSC, 2025b). It runs eight operation centres including the Greater Male' region, serving over half the population, while Fenaka Corporation covers most outer islands. MWSC is also the country's sole domestic manufacturer of PVC and PE pipes, undertaking engineering projects for government and resort clients (MWSC/AGO, 2025).

  • Core purpose: "to help our communities lead a PURE life" (MWSC, 2025b).
  • Audited FY2024: revenue MVR 1.58 billion; net profit MVR 217.5 million (MWSC/AGO, 2025).
  • Business Plan 2026: targets revenue of MVR 1.6 billion and net profit of MVR 186.9 million, and to "build strong inroads toward US dollar revenue streams" while maintaining reliable utility services (MWSC, 2025a).

FY2024 is the latest audited annual report; FY2025 appears as audited comparatives in the Q2 2026 report; in-year 2026 figures are unaudited. All figures are Company rather than Group, for consistency.

2.2 The Strategic Problem

The parallel-market rate recently reached MVR 22.25 per USD, a 44.29% premium above the official MVR 15.42 (Maldives Monetary Authority, 2026), rising steadily in a structural, not temporary, imbalance (Adhadhu, 2026). External debt service reached an estimated USD 1.7 billion in 2026, and official reserves fell from a peak of USD 1.33 billion in March 2026 to USD 717.9 million (1.4 months of import cover) in April after a USD 500 million sukuk repayment and currency swap; gross reserves continued to deteriorate to USD 638.03 million by July 2026 (Maldives Monetary Authority, 2026; Ministry of Finance and Planning, 2026; World Bank, 2026).

MWSC earns in MVR while key inputs (membranes, tanks, pumps, valves, meters) are bought in USD, so it is constantly exposed to currency shortages and rate pressure. Audited FY2024 disclosures show trade receivables of MVR 877.8 million against cash of MVR 50.2 million, a MVR 192.1 million impairment on gross receivables of MVR 1.23 billion flagged as a Key Audit Matter, and net finance costs up to MVR 55.9 million (MWSC/AGO, 2025).

The trajectory confirms this: audited comparatives show FY2025 revenue of MVR 1.37 billion (13% below 2024, only 62% of the MVR 2.2 billion goal) and profit after tax of MVR 146 million, about a third of 2024 (MWSC, 2026). At H1 2026, revenue of MVR 749.3 million was 6.4% below budget, the gap widening from 1.9% (Q1) to 10.9% (Q2).

2.3 Strategic Contribution and Cost of Inaction

Fen Rakkaa is of paramount strategic importance because it directly addresses the misalignment between the currency in which MWSC earns and the currency in which it spends. It is not merely a treasury adjustment; it is a fundamental requirement for the company's financial resilience, for three key reasons:

  • Strategic Alignment: it is the primary vehicle for three goals the Business Plan already sets but has not yet operationalised: USD billing (Goal 2.3.4), a USD share of the cash reserve (Goal 1.5.10) and legal review of foreign-currency contracts (Goal 3.3.2). The intended benefit is a structural reduction in net FX exposure that compounds year on year.
  • Financial Protection: the consequence of no action is an unmanaged annual exposure estimated at MVR 40–70 million (A2), together with the operational risk that USD shortages interrupt imports of chemicals and spares and threaten water supply itself.
  • Capability Building: it creates treasury discipline and contract governance that persist after the project closes and are difficult for others to copy (Barney, 1991), strengthening the financial element of the strategy mix (Callahan and Brooks, 2008).

3.2 Key Issues Analysis

Root-Cause Analysis

An evidence-based diagnosis must separate what is visible from what drives it. The falling margins, tight cash and rising finance costs in Section 2.2 are symptoms; the underlying problem is the structural mismatch between rufiyaa income and dollar costs. Four internal causes sustain that mismatch, in order of priority:

  • Under-developed dollar revenue: the Business Plan records the 150-plus resort sector and international consulting as overlooked markets. Ranked first because dollar income is a natural hedge: every rufiyaa converted to dollars shrinks the net exposure the other three causes each enlarge.
  • No treasury controls: budgets are prepared at the official MVR 15.42, there is no exposure register, and no dollar buffer is held, so the company plans at a rate it cannot transact at.
  • Contract design: project contracts are fixed-price in rufiyaa without FX adjustment clauses, so MWSC silently absorbs currency movements it never priced into the bid.
  • No ownership: no single function owns FX risk, which is why causes 2 and 3 persist. Fixing processes without fixing ownership is single-loop learning; the project targets the governing assumption itself, which is double-loop learning (Argyris, 1977).

Wishbone (Fishbone) Analysis

The causes are structured across six categories: Macroeconomic Environment, Revenue Structure, Financial and Treasury Practices, Procurement and Supply Chain, Contracts and Governance, and Capability and Culture. Only the first sits outside MWSC's control; the remaining five are internal and therefore actionable. The categories are interdependent, so the options in Section 4 are distinguished by how many linked causes each resolves rather than by how much capital each spends (Ishikawa, 1986).

4. Strategic Project Plan

4.1 Project Options and Strategies

Three genuinely distinct options were evaluated, from a status-quo baseline through a do-minimum fix to full transformation. A baseline is included so the value of intervention is tested rather than assumed.

  • Option A: "Do Nothing" (Baseline) — continue budgeting at the official MVR 15.42 and absorb losses. Its true cost is the full A2 exposure; it appears safe while failing every strategic objective, which is why doing nothing is the most expensive option over time.
  • Option B: "The Treasury Fix" (Do-Minimum) — a realistic planning rate, consolidated orders and a basic dollar buffer, ~MVR 1.5 million. Cheap and fast, but revenue stays entirely in rufiyaa, so the root cause is untouched.
  • Option C: "The Full Fen Rakkaa Programme" (Integrated) — six coordinated workstreams covering governance, dollar revenue, treasury, procurement, contracts and energy savings, ~MVR 4.5 million over twelve months under two stage gates. The only option addressing all four root causes; financing is achievable, and the staged approach allows a priced de-scope to Option B if benefits are not realised.

4.2 Financial Appraisal and Sensitivity

Benefits ramp at 15%, 35% and 45% of the MVR 55 million exposure (A2) over three years, against MVR 4.5m one-off and MVR 1.2m annual costs at a 12% discount. Three-year NPV is MVR 33.4 million, benefit-cost ratio 5.8, payback within Year 1. An IRR is not quoted: at this cost scale it would imply false precision (HM Treasury, 2022).

The recommendation is robust to reasonable changes. Doubling the cost weight to 30% and cutting strategic fit to 5% still leaves Option C ranked first (4.00 vs 3.40 and 3.05). The programme breaks even at just 17% of estimated benefits and remains positive at 40% even with running costs over by two-thirds; Gate 2 is set at 35%, above break-even. A change to the peg would strengthen, not weaken, the case for FX governance.

4.3 Recommendation

Option C, the Full Fen Rakkaa Programme, is unequivocally recommended, with Option B retained as a priced fallback should funding be cut. It provides the best available balance of strategic alignment, financial return and risk control, and is the only option that resolves every root cause. The accepted trade-off is additional workload in a crowded change year, managed by delivering through existing KPIs and governance. The principal limitation is that benefits rest on an assumed dollar-linked share of costs rather than published segment data, which is why the exposure register is the first deliverable and the programme is gated rather than committed in full. Because the environment is complex rather than predictable, the design probes, learns and adjusts at each gate (Snowden and Boone, 2007).

6. Conclusion

This proposal has set out a strategically vital project for MWSC. The causes the company can act on are internal, and the recommended Full Programme addresses all of them; the appraisal shows it outperforms both doing nothing and the partial fix on every weighting tested. The logframe and risk register state in advance how delivery will be measured, what failure would look like and what the organisation would do in response. Approval of Option C is therefore requested: a gated investment of roughly 0.3% of annual revenue set against an unmanaged exposure running into the tens of millions.
